ACM SIGCSE TS 2026 · poster

Exploring Bilingual Coding for Inclusive Computer Science Learning

Programming is often introduced as though every learner arrives with the same relationship to English, technical vocabulary, and code syntax. The poster asks a more useful question: what changes when beginners are invited to learn programming through more than one natural language? The work focuses on bilingual coding workshops using the multilingual Hedy platform, a learning environment designed to reduce the jump from readable instructions to formal programming syntax.

In plain terms, bilingual coding does not mean translating a finished program word for word. It means making the route into programming more legible. A learner can discuss an idea in the language that feels most natural, connect that idea to a programming construct, and then gradually practice the conventions of code. That distinction matters because a student may understand sequencing, conditions, or repetition while still feeling shut out by unfamiliar keywords and English-first explanations.

The study examined how workshops shaped student attitudes, perceived understanding, and comfort. Across 60 bilingual participants at a US university—40 with little or no prior programming experience and 20 experienced programmers—all students improved across most measures, and the less experienced group showed the larger gains in confidence and enjoyment. Experienced programmers showed smaller gains and tended to prefer coding in English. Those measures matter because introductory CS is not only about whether a program runs; it is also about whether students can picture themselves as people who belong in the field.

The practical contribution is a reminder for instructors: language support can be part of the learning design, not an accommodation added after the fact. A workshop can preserve rigorous computational thinking while using examples, discussion prompts, and scaffolds that let students reason before they must perform technical English fluently. It also suggests that evaluation should look beyond test scores. Confidence, identity, and willingness to persist can shape whether a learner keeps going long enough to build fluency.

For Yusuf, the project involved survey design and mixed-methods analysis in Professor Ethel Tshukudu’s CS-education lab at SJSU. The result is a grounded case for treating bilingualism as an instructional resource in computer science—not as a deficit to work around.
