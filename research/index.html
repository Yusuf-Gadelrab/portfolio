Research on bilingual coding and adaptive curriculum maps

Plain-language summaries of two accepted ACM SIGCSE 2026 papers co-authored by Yusuf Gadelrab at San José State University.

ACM SIGCSE 2026 · accepted paper

Exploring Bilingual Coding for Inclusive CS Learning

Programming is often introduced as though every learner arrives with the same relationship to English, technical vocabulary, and code syntax. This paper asks a more useful question: what changes when beginners are invited to learn programming through more than one natural language? The work focuses on bilingual coding workshops using the multilingual Hedy platform, a learning environment designed to reduce the jump from readable instructions to formal programming syntax.

In plain terms, bilingual coding does not mean translating a finished program word for word. It means making the route into programming more legible. A learner can discuss an idea in the language that feels most natural, connect that idea to a programming construct, and then gradually practice the conventions of code. That distinction matters because a student may understand sequencing, conditions, or repetition while still feeling shut out by unfamiliar keywords and English-first explanations.

The study examined how workshops shaped student attitudes, perceived understanding, and comfort. Across 60 SJSU participants—40 novice and 20 experienced programmers—the novice group showed the largest gains in confidence and enjoyment. The reported pre-to-post gains in programming confidence, computing identity, enjoyment, and motivation were statistically significant. Those measures matter because introductory CS is not only about whether a program runs; it is also about whether students can picture themselves as people who belong in the field.

The paper’s practical contribution is a reminder for instructors: language support can be part of the learning design, not an accommodation added after the fact. A workshop can preserve rigorous computational thinking while using examples, discussion prompts, and scaffolds that let students reason before they must perform technical English fluently. It also suggests that evaluation should look beyond test scores. Confidence, identity, and willingness to persist can shape whether a learner keeps going long enough to build fluency.

For Yusuf, the project involved survey design and mixed-methods analysis in Professor Ethel Tshukudu’s CS-education lab at SJSU. The result is a grounded case for treating bilingualism as an instructional resource in computer science—not as a deficit to work around.

ACM SIGCSE 2026 · accepted paper

Adaptive Curriculum Maps: Graph-Augmented Retrieval-Oriented LLMs for Education

Students rarely learn a subject in the neat order a textbook implies. They arrive with partial knowledge, gaps, and misconceptions that do not fit a single linear lesson sequence. This paper explores a way to make AI-supported education more sensitive to that reality: represent a curriculum as a map of connected concepts, then use that map to guide what an LLM retrieves and explains.

A curriculum map can be thought of as a graph. Nodes represent ideas such as variables, loops, functions, or prerequisite mathematics; edges represent relationships such as “requires,” “builds on,” or “is commonly confused with.” Instead of asking a language model to answer from a broad and potentially noisy collection of material, a retrieval-oriented system can first locate the concepts most relevant to a learner’s question and their current place in the map. The LLM then has a more disciplined context for producing an explanation, hint, or next step.

The phrase graph-augmented is important. The graph is not merely a visualization for teachers. It provides structure that can constrain and improve retrieval. If a learner struggles with recursion, for example, the system can consider not only the immediate definition but nearby prerequisites, known misconceptions, and follow-on concepts. That creates the possibility of help that is sequenced rather than generic: revisit a prerequisite when needed, reinforce connections, and avoid jumping ahead to an explanation that assumes missing knowledge.

The paper is also timely because educational uses of LLMs need more than fluent answers. A response can sound helpful while being poorly matched to the course, the learner’s readiness, or the instructor’s goals. Retrieval grounded in a curriculum graph offers a concrete design direction: keep the model’s response tied to defined learning relationships and curated instructional material. Instructors retain a way to express what belongs in the curriculum and how concepts depend on one another.

In practice, this approach could support adaptive study plans, targeted practice suggestions, and clearer diagnostic feedback. The central idea is modest but powerful: before an educational assistant generates language, it should know where the learner is trying to go and which conceptual bridges are still missing.
